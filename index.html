Lesson overview

Learning intentions

By the end of the lesson, learners should be able to:

1
Model feeding relationships
Use food chains and food webs to show how energy flows between organisms.
2
Explain ecosystem factors
Distinguish abiotic (non-living) and biotic (living) factors that affect organisms.
3
Predict ecosystem change
Use a food web to predict how a change may affect populations.
Success criteria: I can identify producers and consumers, interpret arrow direction, build a connected food web, and explain at least one cause-and-effect change in a population.

Key vocabulary
Ecosystem — a community of living organisms interacting with their environment.
Abiotic factor — a non-living part of the environment, such as sunlight, water, temperature or soil.
Biotic factor — a living part, such as plants, animals, fungi or bacteria.
Producer — an organism, such as a plant, that makes its own food.
Consumer — an organism that obtains energy by eating other organisms.
Population — a group of the same species living in the same place.
Teacher guide

Suggested lesson sequence

Adjust the timings to fit your class. Use the prompts to guide discussion and check understanding.

0–5 MIN · ENGAGE

Hook: Who depends on whom?

Display a plant, grasshopper, frog and snake. Ask: “If the grass disappeared, which organisms might be affected? Why?” Invite learners to share predictions without correcting them immediately.

Check for: Learners noticing that organisms are connected through feeding relationships.
5–15 MIN · EXPLAIN

Build the foundation

Explain ecosystem, abiotic and biotic factors. Emphasize that most ecosystems receive energy from the Sun; plants capture sunlight through photosynthesis, and animals obtain energy by eating organisms.

Ask: “Is sunlight biotic or abiotic? Is a fungus biotic or abiotic?”
15–25 MIN · MODEL

Food chains and trophic levels

Model a simple chain: grass → grasshopper → frog → snake. Explain that arrows point in the direction energy moves (from the food organism to the organism that eats it).

Ask: “What does the arrow from grass to grasshopper mean?”
25–40 MIN · GUIDED PRACTICE

Connect food chains into a food web

Have groups draw several overlapping food chains. Ask learners to identify producers, primary consumers, secondary consumers and tertiary consumers where applicable.

Teacher move: Ask groups to trace more than one pathway from a producer to a predator.
40–52 MIN · APPLY

What if the ecosystem changes?

Present a drought, flood, temperature increase, new predator or disease scenario. Learners predict which populations may increase or decrease and explain the links in the web.

Prompt: “What evidence in your food web supports your prediction?”
52–60 MIN · EVALUATE

Exit ticket

Learners complete the quiz and one-sentence reflection: “A change in ______ may affect ______ because ______.”

Case study from the presentation

Coral reef ecosystem

Many organisms depend on coral for shelter, food and breeding areas. When ocean temperatures increase, coral bleaching can occur. This can reduce coral populations and affect many species in the food web.

Think–pair–share

  1. What abiotic factor is changing?
  2. How might fewer healthy corals affect organisms that depend on them?
  3. Why could the effect spread through a food web?
Key idea: A change to one factor or population can affect other organisms because species are interconnected.

Printable learner worksheet

Apply what you learned

Name:

  1. Define: What is an ecosystem?
  2. Classify: Give two examples of abiotic factors and two examples of biotic factors.
  3. Model: Draw a food chain with at least four organisms in your notebook. Label the producer and consumers. Remember to point arrows in the direction energy moves.
  4. Explain: If a disease reduces the number of rabbits, how might this affect a predator that eats rabbits? Explain your reasoning.
  5. Case study: Explain one possible effect of coral bleaching on organisms in a coral reef food web.

Teacher assessment notes

Evidence of learning

  • Beginning: Identifies some organisms or factors with support.
  • Developing: Correctly classifies factors and describes a simple food chain.
  • Proficient: Builds a connected food web and explains arrow direction and trophic roles.
  • Extending: Uses food-web links to justify predictions about changes in populations.
